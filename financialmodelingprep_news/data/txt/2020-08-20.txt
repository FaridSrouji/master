Bonds Won’t Cushion Stock Rout, $68 Billion Pension Giant Says. (Bloomberg) -- One of Australia’s largest pension funds aims to invest more than A$1 billion ($718 million) in direct lending to companies, saying highly-priced bonds will struggle to cushion the blow from any stock sell-off.Brisbane-based QSuper recently mandated ADM Capital and is finalizing a deal with Bain Capital to invest in senior, collateralized loans, Chief Investment Officer Charles Woodhouse said by phone. The mandates get similar risk-adjusted returns as buying ports, office buildings and other traditional unlisted assets, he said.It’s part of the A$95 billion pension fund’s plan to shift away from bonds after recently taking profits on Australian and U.S. government debt. With 10-year bond yields below 1%, sovereign debt wouldn’t support the portfolio during any stock sell-off as it has over the past decade, Woodhouse said.“There’s the Covid crisis, there’s geopolitical conflicts around the world, there’s the U.S. election,” he said, pointing to the key drivers of stock market uncertainty. QSuper is investing “as broadly as possible around asset classes where we see return opportunities, but have some ability to diversify against sharp pull backs in the listed equity markets.”Woodhouse’s concern comes as a rally since March lows has pushed the value of global shares to about 20 times their future earnings, the highest in more than 18 years, even as a recession and the Covid-19 pandemic rage.QSuper isn’t alone in stepping up direct lending to generate returns. Construction & Building Unions Superannuation is targeting loans for real estate developers and infrastructure projects like airports that are offering high-single digit returns as it braces for a challenging year ahead. IFM Investors is also expecting to lend to struggling firms from childcare centers to property developers that have exhausted short-term funding methods.ADM and Bain have been given wide discretion to lend in sectors and regions, after initially targeting Asia, Woodhouse said. Initial investments have been in healthcare companies, he said.The push to diversify comes as the fund’s allocation to stocks rose to more than one third of the portfolio in the year to June 30, while it trimmed bond holdings and added to credit, according to disclosures. Still, QSuper holds less stocks and bonds than its public-sector fund peers, according to Australian Prudential Regulation Authority data.“There’s a great deal of uncertainty,” Woodhouse said, justifying the need to diversify investments as much as possible. “If we felt more confident, you’d see more concentration in the portfolio.”For more articles like this, please visit us at bloomberg.comSubscribe now to stay ahead with the most trusted business news source.©2020 Bloomberg L.P.
